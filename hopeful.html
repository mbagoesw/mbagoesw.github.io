About

What is Hopeful: The Black Vein?

Hopeful: The Black Vein is a 2D steampunk platformer set in 1350 London, where a devastating disease called the Black Vein has swept across Europe, leaving victims helpless and marking their bodies with an eerie black color.

You play as Frank Wendell a skilled thief on a desperate mission infiltrating a long abandoned secret lab to steal the only known cure for the disease and save his bedridden sister Ariel. To get there he must navigate through deadly traps, laser grids, automated turrets and other puzzles.

Inspiration

Where It Started

Hopeful: The Black Vein was a final project for a program called Studi Independen where participants studied independently under the guidance of certified industry mentors. One day during the program someone pitched the idea of a steampunk puzzle-platformer and later on i joined his team to help work on it and eventually succeed in finishing the game.

Development

Challenges We Faced

01
Designing puzzles that feel fair

Balancing instant-death obstacles like spikes and lasers with puzzle-solving required careful iteration. Too punishing and players quit; too easy and the tension disappears. Getting that balance right across four levels took a lot of playtesting.

02
Coordinating a six-person team across roles

With two programmers, two artists, one designer, and one producer, keeping everyone aligned on scope, art style, and gameplay feel was a constant challenge. Scope creep and integration issues had to be managed carefully throughout production.

03
Building interactive puzzle mechanics from scratch

Systems like button-activated doors, pushable blocks that interact with lasers, and moving platforms all had to be designed and implemented to work together reliably without breaking in unexpected edge cases.

Reflection

What We Learned

01
Story and atmosphere elevate the whole experience

Having a clear narrative — Frank's desperation to save his sister — gave every design decision more purpose. It made level theming, UI tone, and even the pixel art style feel intentional rather than arbitrary.

02
Puzzle design is its own discipline

Designing puzzles that teach themselves through environment and layout — without explicit instructions — is much harder than it looks. The button-and-box mechanic especially required many iterations before it felt intuitive.

03
A GDD keeps a team honest

Writing a proper Game Design Document before building forced the team to make decisions early — on story, mechanics, and scope — which saved significant time later and gave everyone a shared reference to work from.
